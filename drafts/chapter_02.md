# Chapter 2: Why Games Feel More Real Than Reality

---

## The Problem

You spent six hours building a city. You managed resources, you solved problems, you kept thousands of tiny digital citizens alive and productive. You were focused the whole time. Not the forced focus of studying — the effortless kind where you look up and three hours have passed.

Then you looked at the clock and it was 3 AM, and alongside the guilt about lost sleep there was something else. A recognition, quiet and a little uncomfortable, that the past six hours of game time felt more substantial than the past six months of real life.

Not happier, necessarily. More like your actions had weight. In the game, you knew what you were supposed to be doing. You got feedback on whether you were doing it well. You could see yourself getting somewhere — numbers moving, things unlocking, the world changing in response to your choices.

Compare that to a regular Tuesday. You attended a lecture that felt disconnected from any actual skill. You applied for three things and heard nothing back. You cleaned your apartment and it was messy again within a day. You worked a shift and the money disappeared into rent before you even felt like you'd earned it.

Nothing registered. Nothing moved. Nobody noticed whether you showed up or not.

Of course you prefer the game. The more interesting question is what the game understands about you that the rest of your life seems to have forgotten.

---

## The Definition

The gap between effort and response — that's what I keep coming back to.

In a game, this gap is nearly zero. You press a button, something happens. You complete a task, someone acknowledges it. You improve at something, the numbers prove it. The world responds to you constantly, and the response is usually proportional to what you put in.

In real life, the gap is enormous. You study for weeks and get a letter grade that tells you nothing about what you actually learned. You work hard for a year and receive a performance review that says "meets expectations." You send out applications and get silence back. You put effort into a system that barely registers you exist.

Games close the gap. That's essentially their job. And when you've spent hours in an environment where your actions reliably produce visible results, returning to a world where they don't feels like a kind of emotional whiplash.

Some people call this escapism. I think that's too simple. You're not just running away from real life. You're running toward an environment that treats you like you matter.

---

## The Psychology

In the 1980s, two researchers named Edward Deci and Richard Ryan developed something called Self-Determination Theory. They argued that human motivation — the real kind, the kind where you don't have to force yourself — depends on three things being present.

The first is autonomy: the sense that you're choosing what you do, rather than being directed. The second is competence: the sense that you're capable, improving, building toward mastery at something. The third is relatedness: the sense that you belong somewhere, that other people see you and care what you do.

When all three are present, motivation takes care of itself. You don't need to discipline yourself into doing the thing. You want to do it. You lose track of time. Psychologists call this a flow state — complete absorption in an activity where the challenge fits your skill level and self-consciousness drops away.

A good game provides all three. You choose your character, your path, your playstyle — autonomy. The early levels are easy and the later ones get harder, always matching your growing ability — competence. You play with friends, or compete against people who share your language and goals, or at minimum the NPCs react to your presence — relatedness.

Now consider a typical day. Someone else decided where you needed to be and when. Nobody told you whether you're good at anything that matters to you. You were around people, technically, but almost none of the interactions went deeper than transaction. The barista. The classmate who needed notes. The boss who needed a report.

Games aren't just entertaining you. They're meeting needs that your actual day is starving. Of course you log back in.

There's another layer to this. In a game, progress is broken into tiny visible chunks. You're 47% of the way to the next level. You need 300 more XP. You've completed six of ten quests. Every session shows you a number that moved.

In real life, the things you most want to get better at — being a decent person, building a career, finding relationships that feel real — have no progress indicator. You can work at something for months with no evidence that you're getting anywhere. The feedback cycle is so long it might as well not exist. Motivation doesn't survive that kind of silence for very long.

## The Philosophy

There's a Greek myth about Sisyphus. The gods condemned him to push a boulder up a hill forever, watching it roll back down each time he neared the top. Push, fall, repeat. Eternity as a loop with no completion.

Camus, the French philosopher, used this image to describe modern working life. Wake up, commute, work, eat, sleep. Monday arrives and the same boulder is at the bottom of the same hill. The tasks you finished yesterday need to be done again today. Most of what you accomplish gets undone almost immediately.

Camus's response was strange and beautiful. He said we have to imagine Sisyphus happy — not because the task is meaningful, but because Sisyphus can decide that the struggle itself is enough. He can rebel against the absurdity by choosing it.

I find this hard to live by. Most people do. We're not Camus. We can't just decide that the meaningless feels meaningful. We need help. We need feedback. We need to see the rock stay at the top of the hill at least some of the time.

Games give us that. A hill where progress actually holds.

But there's a catch. The achievements you earn inside a game stay inside the game. A legendary sword in an MMO is real within the rules of that world. It represents genuine effort and skill. But it doesn't make you stronger in the morning. It doesn't make you more capable in a crisis. It doesn't make the people around you know you better.

I don't think this means games are worthless. I think it means the satisfaction they provide, while real, is contained. The needs they meet — autonomy, competence, belonging — are universal human needs. Meeting them inside a fictional world works while you're there. The challenge is that you eventually have to log off.

## Real-Life Examples

Kevin spent years playing League of Legends. He hit Diamond — top two percent of players on his server. That took thousands of hours. He studied strategies, reviewed his own gameplay, practiced mechanics in custom lobbies like someone training for a sport.

People assumed he was addicted. He doesn't see it that way. "I wasn't running from anything. I was running toward something that actually told me how I was doing. Every game gave me numbers — win rate, CS per minute, vision score. I could see myself getting better at something real."

He compared it to his engineering degree. "Four years and I still don't know if I'm good at engineering. My grades say slightly above average. What does that mean? Can I actually build something? Can I solve a problem someone cares about? Nobody tells you. You just keep taking exams and hoping it adds up to something."

He graduated. He's working now. He still plays, though less. "All those hours climbing ranks — none of it transferred. I'm Diamond in a game nobody at my job has heard of, and Bronze in basically everything that affects my actual life. That's not self-hatred. It's just what happened."

Sam is nineteen. She left college after one semester and now spends most of her time in Genshin Impact. She knows how that sounds. "Genshin gives me something school never figured out how to give," she said. "When I explore somewhere new in the game, there's always something there. Treasure, a puzzle, a story. The world rewards curiosity. In school, I was supposed to be curious about subjects that felt disconnected from everything, and the only reward was a slightly better grade. That's not a reward. A grade is a receipt."

Sam isn't lazy. She's memorized the lore of an entire fictional continent, optimized team builds with damage calculation spreadsheets, and internalized resource routes more complicated than any transit map. She has enormous capacity for deep engagement. The target of that engagement just happens to be fictional.

"I know I could direct the same energy at something real if it felt even half as rewarding," she said. "But nothing does. Not because I'm broken. Because nothing else in my life was designed to reward effort the way this game is."

## The Practical Solution

### Step 1: Identify What the Game Is Actually Giving You

Different games meet different needs. Before you change anything, figure out what you're actually getting from the one you keep returning to.

When you finish a gaming session feeling genuinely good — not just distracted, but actually satisfied — what produced that feeling? Was it seeing numbers go up? Pulling off something difficult? Playing with people who know you? Making choices that mattered? Exploring somewhere new and being rewarded for it?

Usually it's a combination. But one need is probably primary. That's the one your offline life is hungry for.

### Step 2: Build One Real-Life Feedback Loop

Don't try to gamify your entire life at once. Pick one area. One thing you actually want to improve at. Build a single simple feedback system around it.

It needs three things: a clear small goal you can finish in one session, immediate feedback on whether you did it, and a way to see yourself accumulating progress over time.

Instead of "learn to code" — which is vague and infinite — try "solve one coding problem today and mark it done." Instead of "get in shape" — same problem — try "run one kilometer and log the time."

This sounds too simple to matter. That's kind of the point. Games feel good because they break massive systems into tiny, feedback-rich pieces. Real life works the same way when you bother to structure it. The difference is that in real life, nobody is going to structure it for you.

### Step 3: Transfer the Real Skills

You've developed actual abilities inside games. Organizing raids, managing group dynamics, researching optimal strategies, teaching new players. These are real skills. They just happen to be aimed at fictional targets.

If you can coordinate twenty people through a complex encounter, you can coordinate a real-world project. If you can theorycraft character builds for hours, you can apply the same analytical energy to understanding how something works — investing, nutrition, career paths, whatever actually interests you.

The capacity is there. You just haven't pointed it at anything outside the game yet.

### Step 4: Flip the Order

Right now, for a lot of you, gaming is the default. It fills whatever space isn't occupied by something mandatory.

Try reversing it. The real-life task comes first — the coding problem, the workout, the creative thing. The game comes after. Not as a reward exactly, but as something you do when you've already done the thing that moves your actual life forward.

This works for a boring psychological reason called the Premack Principle: you can use something you naturally want to do to reinforce something you're trying to build. Parents use this on children. It turns out it works on adults too.

### Step 5: Pick a Build

In an RPG, you don't try to max every stat. You specialize. You decide what kind of character you're playing and you invest accordingly.

In real life, most of us try to be competent at everything simultaneously — career, health, relationships, creative projects, social life, personal development. We spread points so thin that nothing ever reaches a level where it feels satisfying. We're level twelve in fifteen different things, which means we're not actually good at anything, which means nothing gives us that sense of genuine competence.

Pick two or three things. The ones that actually matter. Let the others sit at level one for a while.

---

## Summary

Games don't feel better than reality because you're addicted. They feel better because they were designed — deliberately, by people who understand motivation — to provide the three things humans need to feel engaged: autonomy, competence, and relatedness.

Your actual life was not designed at all. It's a collection of systems that accumulated over time, none of them built with your psychological needs in mind. The gap between effort and feedback is nearly zero in a well-made game. In your average day, it's wide enough to disappear into.

The solution isn't to stop playing. It's to figure out which need your game of choice is fulfilling, build one real-life system that meets the same need, transfer the genuine skills you've developed in gaming, and specialize in a few things rather than spreading yourself across everything.

You're not escaping reality because you're weak. You're spending time in an environment that was built to make you feel competent, autonomous, and connected. The task is to bring some of that design intentionality into the world you actually inhabit.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Gaming Needs Audit
For one week, after each gaming session, write down: what you played, what felt good about it, and which core need it met most — autonomy, competence, or relatedness. At the end of the week, the pattern will tell you what your offline life is missing.

### Exercise 2: Design One Real-Life Quest
Create one quest with a clear objective you can complete in under thirty minutes, a measurable success condition, and something that feels like a reward. Complete one quest per day for five days. Notice whether having a defined quest changes how the task feels.

### Exercise 3: The Skill Transfer
List three skills you've built through gaming. For each, identify one real-world situation where you could apply it this week. Keep it small. "I teach new players mechanics in my MMO" might map to "I could help a friend figure out something they're struggling with."

### Exercise 4: The Character Sheet
Create a real-life character sheet. Pick two or three stats to focus on for the next month. Define what leveling up looks like for each. A phone note is sufficient. The point is clarity about what you're building toward.
