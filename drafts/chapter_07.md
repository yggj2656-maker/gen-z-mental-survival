# Chapter 7: Twenty Dollars a Month

---

## The Problem

You were told a story. It went like this: do well in school. Get into a good college. Pick a marketable major. Build skills. Work hard. The world will reward you. The story was clear. The path was marked. You just had to walk it.

You did those things. Or you're doing them right now — mid-degree, student loans accumulating, watching the story crumble in real time while you're still paying tuition.

In the past two years, AI has gotten good enough to write essays better than you. Faster. Cleaner. No procrastination. No late nights. Code faster than you — in any language, debugging itself as it goes. Generate art. Music. Marketing copy. Legal documents. Financial analysis.

The things you were told would make you valuable — the skills, the credentials, the expertise you spent years building — are being replicated by a machine that costs twenty dollars a month.

Twenty dollars. That's less than a pizza.

You're not just worried about your job prospects. That's part of it, sure. But there's something underneath that — a deeper, more unsettling question.

If a machine can do everything I can do — everything I was told made me valuable, everything I invested in, everything I am — then what am I actually worth?

This isn't really a career anxiety chapter. It's about the thing underneath the career anxiety. The suspicion — quiet, persistent, hard to look at directly — that the AI threat isn't just economic. It's existential. It doesn't just threaten your paycheck. It threatens your understanding of what it means to be human. To be valuable. To matter at all.

---

## The Definition

There are different things people mean when they say they're anxious about AI. They tend to get lumped together, but they're different problems and they need different responses.

Economic anxiety: will AI take my job? This is the most discussed, the most straightforward. It's about income, stability, the practical future. Can I pay rent? Will my field exist in five years? Important questions. But not the deepest layer.

Competence anxiety: AI can do what I do — the thing I trained for, the thing I was proud of — but better. Faster. Cheaper. What was the point of all that effort? All those late nights? All that tuition? This is about the devaluation of your skills and knowledge. The years you spent learning feel retroactively pointless. Like training for a race that got canceled the day before.

Existential anxiety: if machines can think, create, solve problems — the things that were supposed to make humans special — then what's left that's uniquely human? What do I have that a machine doesn't? What am I *for*?

This is the deepest layer. It's not about your job. It's about your species. It's about whether "human" still means anything at all.

Most discussions of AI and Gen Z stay at the first layer. The economic one. "Don't worry, new jobs will emerge." "Learn prompt engineering." "Adapt and upskill." This advice isn't exactly wrong — new jobs probably will emerge, adaptation is probably wise. But it misses most of what's actually going on inside people.

You're not just afraid of being unemployed. You're afraid of being unnecessary.

Those are different fears. One has a solution. The other is harder.

---

## The Psychology

In the 1960s, psychologist Martin Seligman discovered something he called learned helplessness. When animals — and humans — are repeatedly exposed to situations where their actions don't affect the outcome, they stop trying. Even when the situation changes and action would help. They've learned, at a deep level, that effort doesn't matter.

AI has the potential to produce learned helplessness at scale. Not because AI objectively makes effort pointless — it doesn't, not yet, not in most domains. But the *perception* that effort is pointless can become self-fulfilling. If you believe your skills are obsolete, you won't develop them. If you believe effort doesn't matter, you won't put in effort. The belief creates its own evidence.

This is especially dangerous for people still building identity and competence — which is to say, people in their teens and twenties. If you're twenty years old, watching machines outperform professionals with decades of experience, the message your brain receives is: why bother starting at all?

Starting anything requires a belief that effort will eventually produce competence. That the struggle leads somewhere. AI undermines that belief before you've had a chance to test it. You never get to find out that effort works because you never try, because you already believe it won't.

Humans have always compared themselves to other humans. This is what we do. You might be slower than Usain Bolt — but you could still run. You might be worse at math than a mathematician — but you could still calculate. The range of human ability was the entire range. You fell somewhere on it. Wherever you fell, it was still recognizably human territory. You were still in the game.

AI breaks this framework.

For the first time in history, you're comparing yourself to something that isn't human. Something that doesn't get tired. Doesn't have off days. Doesn't need sleep or food or encouragement. Operates at speeds and scales no human brain can approach and never will.

The comparison isn't just unfair — it's the wrong category. Comparing a human to an AI is like comparing a swimmer to a submarine. Both move through water. Both get from point A to point B. But one is a machine purpose-built for the task. The other is a person doing something people do. The frame of comparison is nonsense.

But it doesn't feel like nonsense. It feels devastatingly relevant. Because the output — the essay, the code, the image — looks similar. The AI's output is often better. When output looks similar, your brain assumes the process is similar. And that assumption — even if it's wrong at the level of process — does real damage at the level of feeling.

Psychological well-being depends heavily on a sense of agency — the feeling that you can affect your world — and competence — the feeling that you're good at something that matters. Both are threatened by AI. If a machine can produce in seconds what takes you hours, your agency feels diminished. If a machine can match your best work without trying, your competence feels irrelevant.

But this framing reveals something.

You're defining your worth by your output. By what you produce. By how your product compares to other products. Not by your process. Not by your growth. Not by the experience of doing the thing. By the thing. The artifact. The deliverable.

AI didn't create that equation. The productivist logic — you are what you produce, your value equals your output — was always there. Always fragile. AI just proved it. By producing things faster than you ever could, in more domains than you ever imagined.

The crisis isn't really that AI can do what you do. The crisis is that you never learned to value anything about yourself except your output. And now there's a machine that outproduces you at everything you know how to measure.

If your worth is your output, then AI has made you worthless. And you know — at some level, you know — that can't be right. The math doesn't add up. Something in the equation is wrong.

Before moving on, I want to say something directly.

The AI anxiety you're feeling is not irrational. It's not technophobia. It's not an overreaction. It's not that you don't understand the technology well enough.

For your entire life, you've been told that your mind — your intelligence, your creativity, your ability to learn and solve problems — was the one thing that couldn't be automated. Manual labor got automated in the twentieth century. Factory workers, replaced by machines. But cognitive labor? Thinking? Creating? That was supposed to be safe. That was supposed to be YOUR domain. The one thing the machines couldn't touch.

That was the bet you were told to make: invest in your brain. Develop your mind. That's the one asset that will always be valuable.

Now the machines are coming for the brain. The one thing you were told was safe. The one thing that was supposed to be irreducibly, uniquely human.

The sense of betrayal is real. You played by the rules. You invested where you were told to invest. You did the homework, you got the degree, you built the skills. And now it feels like the game was rigged — or the rules changed without anyone telling you, while you were mid-move.

The anger, the fear, the vertigo — it's not weakness. You're not overreacting. You're reacting appropriately to a world that no longer makes the promise it made to you when you were a kid. The promise was broken. Your response to the broken promise is not the problem.

## The Philosophy

A thought experiment. I keep coming back to this one.

An AI writes a poem about heartbreak. The poem is technically flawless — perfect meter, beautiful imagery, emotional resonance. It's good. Objectively good. A human writes a poem about heartbreak. The poem is rough — the meter stumbles, the imagery is a little cliché, the ending doesn't quite land.

But the human actually had their heart broken. Actually lay in bed at 3 AM unable to sleep. Actually wrote the poem because they needed to, not because someone prompted them. The poem came from somewhere. From a life. From a specific Tuesday night that actually happened.

Is there a difference between these two poems?

If you judge purely by output — by the text on the page, by what the reader experiences — no. The AI poem is better. More polished. More likely to go viral. If you're a publisher deciding what to print, you pick the AI poem.

But if you judge by something other than output — by the fact that one poem emerged from an actual life, from actual suffering, from a person who woke up at 3 AM and couldn't sleep and wrote something down just to get it out of their body — then yes. There's a difference.

The difference is lived experience. The machine processes information. The human processes life. The machine has data — all the poems ever written, all the patterns, all the forms. The human has history — this specific heartbreak, this specific person, this specific 3 AM.

The machine can simulate emotion with terrifying accuracy. The human actually feels it. Whatever "actually feels" means. Whatever the difference is between a simulation of feeling and the feeling itself. I'm not sure I can define it. But I'm pretty sure it matters.

This doesn't make human writing better in any objective sense. It makes human writing human. It comes from somewhere. It means something to the person who made it, even if it means nothing to anyone else. The meaning is in the making, not in the product.

The deepest philosophical challenge of AI is not "what will I do for money?" Important question. Wrong frame. The real question is: what makes a person valuable if not their output?

The answer has existed for centuries. It's been drowned out — by productivity culture, by capitalism, by the voice that says you must earn your place — but it's there. A human being does not derive their worth from what they produce. They derive their worth from being a human being. That's it. Full stop.

This sounds like a platitude. I know it does. But it's the foundational claim of humanism. Of human rights. Of every ethical system worth taking seriously. You have worth that is not contingent on your output. You had it before you produced anything — as a baby, as a child, before you had a job or a degree or anything to show. You will have it after you stop producing. It is not a reward for being useful. It is not earned. It is a property of being alive.

Productivity culture told you the opposite — loudly, repeatedly, in every possible medium. That you must earn your place. That idleness is sin. That value equals output. That you are what you make.

AI didn't invent the lie. AI just made the lie impossible to sustain. Because now there's a machine that can out-produce you at everything. If your worth is your output, then AI has made you worthless — objectively, measurably, on every metric you were taught to care about.

And you know that can't be right. You know, at some level, that a person is not made obsolete by a better text generator. That the equation is wrong. That worth has to be something else.

The AI crisis is an invitation. To finally, definitively, reject the productivist definition of human worth. To replace it with something that was always truer. You matter because you exist. Not because you produce. Because you are.

## Real-Life Examples

Noor, 22, is a computer science student. She was supposed to be on the winning side of this. STEM major. High demand. The future is tech. Instead, she's watching AI code better than she can — and she's good, she's genuinely good, she's been coding since high school.

"I spent three years learning to write code. Data structures. Algorithms. Late nights debugging segfaults. Now Claude can write in any language, debug itself, and produce in thirty seconds what would take me an entire afternoon. It's like I trained to be a switchboard operator the year before smartphones came out. I did everything right. The timing was just... wrong."

She's not dropping out. But she's questioning what any of it means. "If the skill itself is being automated — and fast, faster than anyone predicted — what am I actually learning? What's the point of struggling through algorithms when a machine already knows them better than my professor?"

Her professor gave her an answer that shifted something. "He said: you're not learning to write code. You're learning to think through problems. The code is just the current language that thinking happens in. In ten years it'll be a different language — maybe natural language, maybe something we can't imagine yet. The thinking is what you take with you. The capacity. Not the syntax."

It didn't fix everything. She's still scared. But it reframed the question. The skill isn't the point. The capacity is the point. And capacities transfer.

Dez, 26, is a graphic designer. AI image generation hit his industry directly. Not in the future — right now.

"Clients who used to pay me $500 for a poster now type a prompt into Midjourney and get something 'good enough' for free. They don't care that it's slightly worse — a little wonky, a little generic. They care that it's free. Free beats good. Free beats everything."

The economic anxiety is real and immediate. But what surprised him was the existential layer underneath.

"It's not just the money. It's that I spent ten years getting good at this. Drawing as a kid — that was my thing, I was the kid who could draw. Art school. Late nights learning composition and color theory — really learning it, internalizing it. And now a machine does it in seconds. A machine that doesn't know what a sunset feels like. That's never stayed up all night because the work wasn't right yet."

He paused. "It makes me feel like my entire history of effort was a joke. Like the universe was just waiting for me to finish training so it could reveal that the training was pointless."

He's adapting. Moving into art direction instead of execution, using AI as a tool rather than competing with it. That's the practical move. But the emotional processing, he said, was separate from the career strategy.

"I had to grieve. Not grieve my income — grieve the meaning of my craft. I had to separate 'I make art' from 'I am an artist.' The first one changed overnight. The second one didn't have to. But I had to learn that. I had to feel the difference."

## The Practical Solution

### Step 1: Separate the Three Anxieties

Write down your AI fears. All of them — the big ones, the petty ones, the ones that sound stupid when you say them out loud. Sort them into three columns: economic, competence, existential.

Economic fears are practical. They need practical responses: skill adaptation, career flexibility, financial planning. Real problems with real — if uncertain — solutions. Treat them practically.

Competence fears are about the meaning of your effort. They need reframing: the value of learning isn't just the skill you acquire — it's the capacity you build. Learning to write isn't just about producing essays. It's about learning to think — to structure an argument, to follow a thought to its conclusion. The skill may become obsolete. The capacity doesn't. Capacities transfer. Skills expire.

Existential fears are about what makes you human. They need a different kind of response — not practical, not strategic. Philosophical. The response is: you matter because you exist. Not because you produce. Because you are.

### Step 2: Identify What AI Cannot Do For You

AI can simulate many things. Impressively, scarily well. But there are things it cannot be.

The specific person who went through your specific life. The friend who remembers that conversation from three years ago — the one that changed things between you. The partner who knows how you take your coffee and that you get quiet when you're actually upset, not angry. The person who shows up. Physically. In a room. With a body.

AI cannot be present in the way a human is present. It cannot witness in the way a human witnesses. It cannot care in the way a human cares — it can simulate care, convincingly, but you can't be cared for by a simulation any more than you can be fed by a picture of food. The simulation is not the thing.

Your irreplaceability is not in your output. It's in your presence. In the fact that you are *this* person — this specific configuration of history and memory and relationship — showing up for *these* people in ways no machine ever could. Even if the machine writes better. Even if the machine codes faster. None of that replaces what you are to the people who know you.

### Step 3: Redefine Worth Independent of Output

This is the hardest step. And the most important. You need to genuinely, practically — not just intellectually, in your body, in your life — believe that your worth is not your output.

Spend time with people who don't care what you produce. Who don't ask what you're working on. Who don't evaluate you by your metrics. Do things that produce nothing. Be useless for an afternoon. Be unproductive on purpose. Notice that you're still alive at the end of it. Still valuable to the people who love you. Still a person.

If this feels uncomfortable — if doing nothing makes you feel guilty or worthless or panicky — that's the productivist programming. It was installed before you had a choice. You absorbed it from the culture, from school, from your parents who absorbed it from their parents. It's not yours. You can uninstall it. But uninstalling takes practice. Being useless is a skill you have to rebuild.

### Step 4: Make Things Anyway

The fact that AI can make something better than you is not a reason not to make it. This feels counterintuitive but I think it's true.

You don't make things because you're the best at making them. You make them because making is part of being human. Children draw not because their drawings are good — they're terrible, objectively, they're scribbles — but because drawing is fun. Because making marks on paper feels good. They don't compare their scribbles to the Mona Lisa and conclude they should stop. The comparison doesn't occur to them. They're not drawing to compete. They're drawing to draw.

AI is the Mona Lisa of everything. It will always be better than you at everything, eventually — faster, cleaner, more polished. If that's your reason not to do something, you'll never do anything again. So the reason has to change.

Make things because making connects you to yourself. Because the process of creating — not the quality of the output, but the *experience* of making — is inherently valuable. Because you're a human and humans make things. We always have. Not to win. Not to be the best. Because making is part of living.

### Step 5: Double Down on Being Human

The things that will matter most in an AI-saturated world are the things AI cannot simulate well. Presence. Relationship. Embodiment. Care. Touch. Being there — physically, actually there. Showing up. Loving someone in particular — not love in the abstract, but loving THIS person, the one with the weird laugh and the habit of leaving cabinets open. Knowing someone in particular — not data points, not a profile, but the accumulated knowledge of years in the same room.

These are not "soft skills." They're not the consolation prize for people who can't code. They're the actual substance of a human life. They always were. The rest — the output, the productivity, the credentials, the marketable skills — was always secondary. AI just made that obvious.

AI stripped away everything that wasn't essential and what's left is: each other. That's what we have. That's what we've always had.

Double down on connection. On showing up. On being present with people in physical space — same room, same air, same silence between words. On knowing and being known. The economic future is genuinely uncertain — anyone who tells you they know what jobs will exist in twenty years is lying. But the human future belongs to people who remember what humans actually need. Which is each other. It's always been each other.

## Summary

AI didn't create the crisis of meaning. It exposed a crisis that was already there. You were told your worth was your output. Now there's a machine that can output more than you, faster, cheaper, in more domains. If the productivist equation were correct, you'd be obsolete.

But you know — you know — that you're not obsolete. The thing that needs to change isn't your output. It's the equation you're using to calculate your worth.

Psychologically, AI can trigger learned helplessness by making effort seem pointless before you've had a chance to discover that effort works. It creates a comparison frame that was never fair — human versus machine, swimmer versus submarine. The resolution isn't to compete harder. You'll lose that competition every time. The resolution is to recognize that the frame is wrong. You're not a slower machine. You're a different category of thing.

Philosophically, AI is an invitation — forced, unwelcome, but real — to reject the productivist definition of human worth. To replace it with something truer. You matter because you exist. Your irreplaceability is in your presence. Your relationships. Your specific history with specific people. Not in your ability to produce. In your ability to be there.

Machines can produce. Only you can be you.

## Exercises

### Exercise 1: The Three-Column AI Fear
Take fifteen minutes. Write down every AI-related fear you have — the big ones, the small ones, the ones that sound embarrassing. Sort into three columns: economic (money/job), competence (skills/effort), existential (meaning/identity). For each column, write one concrete action you can take this week. Not a solution. An action.

### Exercise 2: The "AI Can't" List
Make a list of things AI cannot do for you, specifically. Not "AI can't love" — too vague. "AI can't be the friend who knows I get anxious before presentations and texts me beforehand without being asked." "AI can't be the person my younger sister calls when she needs advice about something she can't tell our parents." Be specific. The list will be longer than you expect.

### Exercise 3: Create Something Bad on Purpose
Make something this week — write, draw, build, cook — with the explicit goal of it being bad. Not shared. Not judged. Not posted. Just made. Notice: was the experience of making still meaningful, even though the output was deliberately terrible? If so, your relationship to creation survives the quality of the product. That's worth knowing.

### Exercise 4: The Useless Afternoon
Spend an entire afternoon doing things that produce nothing of value. No productivity. No optimization. No output anyone would pay for. Walk somewhere without a destination. Talk to a friend about nothing important. Sit on a bench and watch people go by. At the end, ask: are you still here? Still a person? Still mattering to the people in your life? If yes — and you are — then your worth was never about your output. It was about something else. Something a machine can't touch.
